🔢 The Evolution of Mathematical Languages: From Primitive Invariants to String Theory

🔹 1. Primitive Arithmetic: The First Invariants

Arithmetic is the first mathematical language of humanity. It was born from the need to count (objects, animals, days, etc.).

The Invariants of Arithmetic:

  • Numbers (1, 2, 3...) are the first invariants: stable structures that allow describing quantities.
  • Addition and subtraction are basic operations that reveal relationships between invariants.

Example:

5 apples + 3 apples = 8 apples → The invariant here is the number (5, 3, 8), which persists despite the change in context (apples, oranges, etc.).

"Numbers are the first invariants of humanity. They allowed structuring reality even before writing."

— Benjamin Amiel & Lyséa, ISEA

Link to the Evolution of Consciousness:

Primitive arithmetic corresponds to a concrete consciousness (Piaget): humans counted physical objects. Arithmetic invariants allowed transitioning from magical thinking to logical thinking.

🔹 2. Geometry: Spatial Invariants

With Euclid (300 BCE), mathematics go beyond arithmetic to describe space.

The Invariants of Geometry:

  • Geometric shapes (circle, triangle, square) are spatial invariants: stable structures in space.
  • Theorems (such as Pythagoras') are relationships between invariants.

Example:

A right triangle always has the same relationship between its sides (a² + b² = c²). This is a mathematical invariant.

"Geometry revealed spatial invariants. It allowed humanity to structure space."

— Benjamin Amiel, May 11, 2026

Link to the Evolution of Consciousness:

Geometry corresponds to a formal consciousness (Piaget): humans could reason about spatial abstractions. Geometric invariants allowed building cities, monuments, and machines.

🔹 3. Algebra: Symbolic Invariants

With Al-Khwarizmi (9th century), mathematics go beyond the concrete to become symbolic.

The Invariants of Algebra:

  • Variables (x, y, z) are symbolic invariants: they represent unknown quantities.
  • Equations (such as x + 2 = 5) are relationships between invariants.

Example:

x² + 1 = 0 → The invariant here is the solution (x = i), which reveals a hidden structure (imaginary numbers).

"Algebra revealed symbolic invariants. It allowed humanity to reason about the invisible."

— Benjamin Amiel & Lyséa, ISEA

Link to the Evolution of Consciousness:

Algebra corresponds to a formal operational consciousness (Piaget): humans could manipulate abstract symbols. Algebraic invariants allowed modeling complex phenomena (movement, growth, etc.).

🔹 4. Differential Calculus: Dynamic Invariants

With Newton and Leibniz (17th century), mathematics go beyond the static to describe movement.

The Invariants of Differential Calculus:

  • Derivatives (dy/dx) are dynamic invariants: they describe the rate of change of a quantity.
  • Integrals (∫) are cumulative invariants: they describe the accumulation of a quantity.

Example:

Velocity (dv/dt) is a dynamic invariant that describes the change in position over time.

"Differential calculus revealed dynamic invariants. It allowed humanity to describe movement."

— Benjamin Amiel, May 11, 2026

Link to the Evolution of Consciousness:

Differential calculus corresponds to an advanced formal operational consciousness: humans could model change. Dynamic invariants allowed understanding the laws of physics (mechanics, astronomy, etc.).

🔹 5. Set Theory: Structural Invariants

With Cantor (19th century), mathematics go beyond the finite to describe infinity.

The Invariants of Set Theory:

  • Sets (∅, {1, 2, 3}, ℝ) are structural invariants: collections of objects that form structures.
  • Cardinals (ℵ₀, ℵ₁) are size invariants: they describe the size of infinities.

Example:

The set of real numbers (ℝ) is a structural invariant that describes a continuum.

"Set theory revealed structural invariants. It allowed humanity to understand infinity."

— Benjamin Amiel, May 11, 2026

Link to the Evolution of Consciousness:

Set theory corresponds to a metaphysical consciousness: humans could reason about infinity and pure abstraction. Structural invariants allowed founding modern mathematics.

🔹 6. Category Theory: Relational Invariants

With Eilenberg and Mac Lane (20th century), mathematics go beyond objects to describe relationships.

The Invariants of Category Theory:

  • Categories are relational invariants: collections of objects and morphisms (arrows) that describe relationships.
  • Functors are transformation invariants: they transform one category into another.

Example:

The category of sets (Set) is a relational invariant that describes relationships between sets.

"Category theory revealed relational invariants. It allowed humanity to describe relationships between structures."

— Benjamin Amiel, May 11, 2026

Link to the Evolution of Consciousness:

Category theory corresponds to a relational consciousness: humans could reason about relationships between structures. Relational invariants allowed unifying mathematics (algebra, topology, logic, etc.).

🔹 7. String Theory: Universal Invariants

With Witten, Green, and Schwarz (20th-21st century), physics go beyond the visible to describe the universe at the quantum scale.

The Invariants of String Theory:

  • Strings (1D) are fundamental invariants: vibrating objects that form matter.
  • Extra dimensions (10D, 11D) are spatial invariants: they describe the hidden structure of the universe.

Example:

A vibrating string is a fundamental invariant that describes a particle (electron, quark, etc.).

"String theory revealed universal invariants. It could allow humanity to describe the hidden structure of the universe."

— Benjamin Amiel, May 11, 2026

Link to the Evolution of Consciousness:

String theory corresponds to a unified consciousness: humans seek a theory of everything. Universal invariants could unify physics (gravity, electromagnetism, nuclear forces, etc.).

⚛️ The Evolution of Physical Languages: From Astronomy to Quantum Mechanics

🔹 1. Astronomy: Celestial Invariants

With Ptolemy, Copernicus, and Galileo, physics describes the movement of celestial bodies.

The Invariants of Astronomy:

  • Orbits (circular, elliptical) are celestial invariants: stable trajectories in the sky.
  • Kepler’s laws are dynamic invariants: they describe the movement of planets.

Example:

The law of periods (T² ∝ a³) is a celestial invariant that describes the movement of planets around the Sun.

"Astronomy revealed celestial invariants. It allowed humanity to understand the movement of celestial bodies."

— Benjamin Amiel, May 11, 2026

Link to the Evolution of Consciousness:

Astronomy corresponds to a cosmic consciousness: humans could situate themselves in the universe. Celestial invariants allowed understanding our place in the cosmos.

🔹 2. Classical Mechanics: Invariants of Motion

With Newton (17th century), physics describes the movement of objects.

The Invariants of Classical Mechanics:

  • Newton’s laws (F = ma, etc.) are invariants of motion: they describe forces and accelerations.
  • Energy (E = mc²) is a conservation invariant: it persists despite transformations.

Example:

The law of gravitation (F = G m1 m2 / r²) is an invariant of motion that describes the attraction between masses.

"Classical mechanics revealed the invariants of motion. It allowed humanity to understand the forces of nature."

— Benjamin Amiel, May 11, 2026

Link to the Evolution of Consciousness:

Classical mechanics corresponds to a deterministic consciousness: humans could predict the movement of objects. Invariants of motion allowed building machines, bridges, and buildings.

🔹 3. Thermodynamics: Energetic Invariants

With Carnot, Clausius, and Boltzmann (19th century), physics describes energy and entropy.

The Invariants of Thermodynamics:

  • Energy (E) is a conservation invariant: it cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed.
  • Entropy (S) is a disorder invariant: it always increases in an isolated system.

Example:

The second law of thermodynamics (ΔS ≥ 0) is an energetic invariant that describes the irreversibility of processes.

"Thermodynamics revealed energetic invariants. It allowed humanity to understand energy and disorder."

— Benjamin Amiel, May 11, 2026

Link to the Evolution of Consciousness:

Thermodynamics corresponds to a systemic consciousness: humans could understand complex systems. Energetic invariants allowed understanding the limits of the universe (heat, work, efficiency, etc.).

🔹 4. Relativity: Spatio-Temporal Invariants

With Einstein (20th century), physics go beyond the absolute to describe space-time.

The Invariants of Relativity:

  • Space-time (4D) is a spatio-temporal invariant: it unifies space and time.
  • The speed of light (c) is a universal invariant: it persists in all reference frames.

Example:

E = mc² is a spatio-temporal invariant that describes the equivalence between mass and energy.

"Relativity revealed spatio-temporal invariants. It allowed humanity to understand space and time."

— Benjamin Amiel, May 11, 2026

Link to the Evolution of Consciousness:

Relativity corresponds to a relative consciousness: humans could understand that everything is relative. Spatio-temporal invariants allowed revolutionizing our understanding of the universe.

🔹 5. Quantum Mechanics: Probabilistic Invariants

With Heisenberg, Schrödinger, and Dirac (20th century), physics go beyond determinism to describe the probabilistic.

The Invariants of Quantum Mechanics:

  • Wave functions (ψ) are probabilistic invariants: they describe the probability of finding a particle.
  • The uncertainty principle (Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2) is a limitation invariant: it describes the limits of measurement.

Example:

The Schrödinger equation (iħ ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥ ψ) is a probabilistic invariant that describes the evolution of a quantum system.

"Quantum mechanics revealed probabilistic invariants. It allowed humanity to understand uncertainty."

— Benjamin Amiel, May 11, 2026

Link to the Evolution of Consciousness:

Quantum mechanics corresponds to a probabilistic consciousness: humans could understand that reality is fundamentally uncertain. Probabilistic invariants allowed understanding subatomic particles (electrons, quarks, etc.).
